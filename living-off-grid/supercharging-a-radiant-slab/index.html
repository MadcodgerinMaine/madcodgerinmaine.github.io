Supercharging a Radiant Slab with Off-Grid Solar

On sunny winter days we turn surplus solar electricity into heat stored in our concrete radiant slabs. The buildings then coast through the night, and often through several cloudy days, on very little propane. Here is how we do it, using two different heat sources in two buildings.

The setup

We live off grid in Maine with no utility connection. Each building has its own PV array and battery bank. A generator is the last resort, and we try hard not to use it. We got through the past two winters without it, though that was before we added an air-to-water heat pump and an electric vehicle.

Both buildings have hydronic radiant tubing in 6-inch concrete slabs. Each slab sits on R10 foam, with R10 around all four edges and another four feet of R10 extending horizontally out from the slab. The main house has R43 walls and an R65 ceiling. The studio is a little less, but not far off.

The idea

A radiant slab is a big battery. It just stores heat instead of electricity. Most people with a radiant slab never use it that way, because they pay for every unit of energy that goes into it. Solar owners are in a different position:  Free energy falls from the sky, even on cold winter days. Grid-tied owners can bank that surplus as utility credits. Off grid, we have to find somewhere else to put it, and the slab is a very good place.

So once the electric batteries are full (or nearly so) and the EV has enough charge for the day’s driving, we push the extra energy into the slabs. Thanks to its thermal mass, a slab absorbs heat slowly and releases it slowly, for hours or even days. A well-insulated building doesn’t overheat; it just stays comfortable. Capture enough and you can skip the boiler entirely. I call heating the slab a few degrees past what the building needs right now “supercharging” the slab. It can cut a heating bill, and fossil fuel use, significantly.

The rule we never break

Heat storage must never drain the electric batteries. Every session stops early if the batteries start to suffer, and always well before sunset. By choice we have no grid to fall back on, and we don’t like running the generator, so Home Assistant watches the batteries closely and always puts them ahead of the slab.

The controller

Home Assistant runs both buildings with the same three-part pattern:

  1. ON conditions. Every 5 minutes during daylight, check battery state of charge, charging rate, and the day’s solar forecast. If there is real surplus, set a “solar for radiant heat” flag for that building.
  2. Actions. When the flag turns on, raise the thermostat setpoints and the heat source’s water temperature. When it turns off, put everything back to normal.
  3. OFF conditions. End the session if any of these happen:
    • Batteries drop below 60% (a number we’re still tuning)
    • Batteries discharge hard for 5 minutes
    • The forecast shows no more spare solar
    • PV output falls too low after noon
    • It is 45 minutes before sunset

Main house:  Mitsubishi Ecodan air-to-water heat pump

Why we moved beyond resistance heat

Last winter the main house used the electric elements in its buffer tank to displace propane, the same approach the studio still uses (see below). It worked. In February 2026, not a warm month in Maine, we burned propane on exactly one day while keeping the garage at 60°F or better.

But those elements draw about 7,000 W. In November and December, with far fewer sunny days, there isn’t enough sun to run the house and feed that load regularly. We needed more heat per watt.

We already had Mitsubishi air-to-air heat pumps in the living space upstairs and were very happy with them. There was one in the garage too, but a wall unit can’t do what a warm slab does:  Drive in at dusk covered in snow and slop, and by morning the floor is dry. Only a heated slab does that.

There are two practical ways to run a slab on a heat pump:  Geothermal, which costs a fortune up front, or air-to-water. When we built in 2024, the air-to-water units available here didn’t meet our needs. A friend who is a Mitsubishi wholesaler told me the Ecodan might reach the US market in 2026. I knew it from overseas forums, where owners praised its efficiency, its sub-zero performance, and the Mitsubishi reliability we already trusted. When he called to say it was coming, I ordered one of the first units installed in Maine, and my HVAC contractor took one of the first local training classes. We installed it this past summer and waited for cold weather.

Water temperature

Normally we run 95°F water through the slabs. That is enough to heat these buildings through a Maine winter, because we designed them for efficiency from the start. Friends with hydronic slabs run much hotter water or water-glycol mixes. Most of them, in a reasonably well-insulated building, could probably turn it down and save money without noticing any difference.

Supercharging is different. You need to move as much heat as possible during a few hours of sun. So during a “solar session,” our name for a supercharging period, we raise the Ecodan’s target from 95°F to 109°F. We do this through an add-on Wi-Fi module from ProtoArt, a company in the Netherlands. You could build the equivalent with an ESP32, but ProtoArt’s module is proven by many users, works natively with Home Assistant, and I was in a hurry.

Zones and short cycling

The system has two zones. The large zone is four loops in an oversized two-car garage. The small zone is two short loops in the entry foyer and a full bath. A 26-gallon buffer tank helps, but with 109°F water and only the small zone calling, the Ecodan still wants to short-cycle. Home Assistant prevents most of that by opening the garage loops when the foyer is about to be satisfied, even if the garage doesn’t strictly need heat (details below). A mixing valve set just below 110°F protects the slab.

How a session runs

  1. Start. House batteries are full, or have averaged more than 6,000 W of charging for 15 minutes. The forecast (a model I built with Claude) shows more than 5 kWh of spare solar after the batteries refill. Batteries are above 60%, and it is at least 1.5 hours before sunset.
  2. Actions. Foyer thermostat to 70°F and Ecodan water to 109°F through the ProtoArt module. Garage held at 60°F to start (see staging below).
  3. Stop. After noon with PV below 3,000 W for 10 minutes, or any battery rule from the overview, or 45 minutes before sunset. Foyer back to 67°F, garage to 60°F, Ecodan to 95°F. A safety-net reset runs 40 minutes before sunset in case nothing else fired.

On cloudy days, or when the electricity is needed elsewhere, the Ecodan still heats the same zones at 95°F. We just don’t supercharge. If the energy picture gets worse than that, Home Assistant tells the Ecodan to take a break and the propane tankless heater takes over. If our modeling is right, that will happen only a few days a year, about 6.5 gallons of propane for an entire Maine winter. Time will tell.

Why we stage the garage zone

On a November day (37–59°F outside during the session, 25°F the night before) we saw two problems:

  • Both zones open:  The compressor ran at 70–72 Hz early, then held at exactly 58 Hz once it was above about 42°F outside. The slab absorbed heat as fast as the Ecodan made it, and the water stalled at 95–96°F. Fine for heating the building, useless for supercharging.
  • Foyer only:  Even the compressor’s minimum speed (about 26 Hz on a mild day) made more heat than the small zone could take. Water overshot to 110–111°F and the compressor shut off for a few minutes. That looked like short cycling to us, buffer tank or not.

The fix:  Start with the foyer only, so the buffer tank heats quickly. When the buffer core passes 105°F, open the garage zone too (setpoint 70°F). If the core drops below 98°F after the garage has been open at least 60 minutes, close it again. A daily counter in Home Assistant allows at most three garage openings per day, since each one cycles four thermal actuators. After the third, the garage simply stays open until the session ends. And if the garage calls for heat on its own, it gets it regardless.

Open questions

  • Energy reporting. The ProtoArt “energy consumed” counter didn’t increase while the Ecodan ran, so we don’t rely on it yet. There is no flow meter, so heat output and COP aren’t measured. I’d like to add that eventually; I really want to know our COP on an ongoing basis.

Studio:  “Old school” electric elements in the buffer tank

The studio keeps the system we used in the house last winter to prove the concept. We considered an Ecodan here too, but couldn’t justify the cost for a building that sometimes sits unused for days and isn’t our residence. The studio’s primary heat is a propane boiler feeding a buffer tank, plus a wood stove we light a few times a year, partly for the ambiance and partly because we like the feel of wood heat.

The buffer tank’s electric elements can supply enough heat for this smaller building without running at full output. Each element has three internal loops, and you can disconnect one or two to reduce output. I pulled two loops out of the top element, which dropped total output from about 7,000 W to about 4,600 W (bottom element full, top element on one loop). We also added about 4,000 W of PV to the studio roof in summer 2026. Because the studio uses far less electricity than the house, that is plenty to run a less efficient heat source on most sunny days.

A two-stage aquastat ties it together:  On sunny days the elements heat the tank and the boiler stays off. Otherwise the elements stay off and the boiler runs. As long as the tank is hot enough, the boiler never fires.

How a session runs

  1. Start. Every 5 minutes, there must be enough spare solar in the forecast (or, if the forecast is unavailable, the panels must be carrying the elements right now). It also needs at least 3 kW of PV or a full battery, which stops early-morning on/off cycling. No new session starts in the last 1.5 hours before sunset.
  2. Actions. Save the current thermostat setpoint, turn the elements on, and send a notification.
  3. Thermostat follows the tank. The slab thermostat goes to 72°F only once the tank core reaches 115°F. If the core stays below 95°F for 3 minutes, the thermostat returns to its saved setpoint, because the boiler fires near 90°F. This keeps the slab drawing solar heat, not propane.
  4. Tank limit. Elements pause at 130°F and resume at 125°F. A separate 135°F safety automation can shut them down if needed.
  5. Stop. After noon, if studio PV can’t cover the elements plus the building’s loads for 10 minutes; or the spare solar is used up; or the battery is below 60% or discharging more than 2 kW for 5 minutes; or the room is above 72°F; or it is 45 minutes before sunset. Elements off, thermostat back to its saved setting (65°F if unknown).

Was it worth it?

If saving money is your only test, probably not. Propane isn’t that expensive, the Ecodan wasn’t cheap, and this took real time to set up.

But money wasn’t the goal. We wanted to heat our buildings without depending on outside energy, not even on cutting wood if it came to that. The propane truck making it up our driveway in a hard Maine winter, and the long-term availability of propane itself, are dependencies we wanted to shrink. We also wanted to cut our fossil fuel use substantially. This system does both. We can now heat the house and the studio for almost nothing across an entire winter.

As I told my wholesaler friend:  I can make electricity right here on the property, but I can’t make propane. And there is a lot to be said for looking out at a cold Maine day, turning the heat up a notch, and knowing it won’t cost a penny more.
